Abstract

Neural Biome is an interactive experiment extending Sakana AI's Digital Ecosystems: a population of learned neural cellular species compete, adapt, and reorganize on a shared grid. The central question is whether six visually and dynamically distinct regimes can be arranged along a single scalar coordinate φ ∈ [0, 1], analogous in spirit to Langton's λ for classical cellular automata.

φ is not an arbitrary reparameterization; it is a smooth, calibrated path through three interpretable controls—growth-gate steepness, competition temperature, and survival threshold—chosen such that each waypoint corresponds to a mechanistically distinct dynamical regime. The central contribution is a reduction: a learned ecosystem that appears high-dimensional can be rendered legible through one carefully designed experimental trajectory.

This article presents the update rule, the learned attack-defense fields, the calibrated φ path, the six regime definitions, and the iterative protocol used to discover the final parameter ordering. The hypothesis is intentionally falsifiable: if any two regimes collapse into a single dynamical class under independent measurement, the one-dimensional claim is weakened.

Background and Motivation

Multi-agent artificial life systems are easy to observe and difficult to explain. A viewer can recognize territorial expansion, invasion fronts, population collapse, rapid flickering, or sustained coexistence, yet these observations remain anecdotal without a controlled experimental frame. Sakana AI's Digital Ecosystems demonstrates that learned neural species can generate rich competitive dynamics; Neural Biome asks whether part of that richness can be compressed into a single interpretable coordinate, and what the structure of such a compression reveals about the mechanisms of the underlying system.

The precedent is Langton's λ parameter for classical cellular automata, which organized a spectrum from frozen to chaotic computation along a single scalar. λ succeeded not because it captured every detail of rule space, but because it identified a regime boundary—the edge of chaos—where computationally interesting behavior concentrated. Neural Biome applies a cognate strategy to a learned, adaptive setting: rather than rule tables, the control surface is defined by three continuous parameters of a differentiable update rule, and the question is whether a designed path through that surface can recover similarly structured regime transitions.

System

The environment is a two-dimensional toroidal grid supporting five species. Each cell stores a scalar aliveness value per species. At each timestep, a cell reads its 3×3 neighborhood, aggregates local species concentrations into a pooled signal, constructs a defense environment vector from those concentrations, scores each candidate species by its attack-defense affinity against that environment, and updates aliveness through a growth gate applied to the resulting competition weights.

Every species at every cell carries two learned vectors: an attack vector and a defense vector. Both are initialized near fixed species identities and are updated online via TensorFlow.js gradient steps during the simulation. The ecosystem is therefore adaptive in two coupled senses: aliveness distributions change as competition proceeds, and the local competitive representations co-evolve with those distributions.

The competition step assigns normalized species weights via temperature-scaled softmax:

(1) wi(x,y) = exp(scorei(x,y) / τ) ∑j exp(scorej(x,y) / τ)

The growth gate applies a sigmoidal participation filter to the pooled neighborhood signal:

(2) gi(x,y) = σ(kgate · (pooledi(x,y) − θ))

The temperature τ governs the sharpness of competitive exclusion: as τ → 0 the softmax collapses to a winner-take-all argmax; as τ increases, weight is distributed more uniformly across species. kgate controls the steepness of the alive/dead boundary, and θ is the minimum pooled concentration required for a species to hold positive participation weight. These three quantities constitute the coordinate system through which φ traces its path.

Adaptive Dynamics

The learned engine is deliberately decoupled from any hard-coded regime classifier. The same update rule governs all six regimes; distinct behavior emerges solely because the parameters place the ecosystem under structurally different competitive pressures. No conditional branching injects regime-specific dynamics.

The learning objective rewards attack vectors that align with the local defense environment, penalizes vector-norm collapse, and applies a weak diversity pressure to prevent the ecosystem from degenerating trivially into a monoculture. A critical calibration decision concerned the strength of that diversity term: an aggressive fairness correction prevented extinction but simultaneously erased competitive asymmetries, depriving the system of genuine winners and losers. The final formulation applies ecological rescue—a survival floor below which a species receives marginal support—rather than equality enforcement. Under this regime, species can lose locally, recover globally, or be competitively eliminated, depending on the interaction dynamics at each waypoint.

The φ Path

Neural Biome operationalizes φ by six calibrated waypoints in (kgate, τ, θ) space, connected by monotone cubic Hermite interpolation. The interpolant is smooth, overshoot-free, and ensures that the slider traverses a continuous experimental trajectory rather than toggling between disconnected presets.

The path is non-monotonic in each individual parameter, and that non-monotonicity is itself a finding. kgate decreases from Frozen toward Chaotic, then rises for Cooperative. τ follows a pronounced U-shaped curve: low competition temperature produces hard local winners and the temporal flickering associated with rapid rank reversal, while elevated temperature softens selection pressure sufficiently to permit stable coexistence. θ modulates whether weakly concentrated species retain participation rights in local competition. Taken together, the curve's shape encodes a structural claim: stable cooperation is not the natural continuation of chaos at higher φ; it occupies a geometrically distinct region of the control surface and requires an explicit turn in parameter space.

Six Regimes

Each regime is defined operationally by its dynamical signature, not by its visual appearance alone. A regime is accepted only when its failure mode is distinguishable from its neighbors under the same update rule. The explore buttons reset the simulation at the corresponding φ waypoint.

Frozen φ = 0.00

kGate = 20.00 · τ = 1.00 · threshold = 0.55

High gate sharpness and a strict participation threshold suppress meaningful frontier contact. Seeded islands persist indefinitely, but diversity is conserved by initialization rather than by ongoing competitive interaction—the system is not yet an ecology.

Territorial φ = 0.27

kGate = 3.45 · τ = 0.32 · threshold = 0.53

Species expand until fronts meet, then settle into coherent, durable domains. Borders may be soft, but region interiors remain spatially legible; early movement relaxes into stable structure rather than continuing to evolve.

Edge of Chaos φ = 0.43

kGate = 2.65 · τ = 0.44 · threshold = 0.49

Stable cores persist under continued competitive pressure from active, moving fronts. The regime is distinguished from Territorial by sustained frontier motion and from Chaotic by the preservation of spatially coherent interiors.

Flicker-Mixing φ = 0.65

kGate = 1.55 · τ = 0.09 · threshold = 0.38

Near-zero temperature drives the softmax toward winner-take-all selection. The visually mixed field is not cooperative coexistence; it is rapid temporal switching as cells commit to successive local winners and reverse as the competitive field shifts.

Chaotic φ = 0.85

kGate = 0.95 · τ = 1.75 · threshold = 0.43

Transient cooperative-looking structures emerge but fail to persist. The defining signature is the continual loss of long-lived spatial organization: borders dissolve, partially reform, and dissolve again under sustained competitive instability.

Cooperative φ = 1.00

kGate = 2.10 · τ = 1.35 · threshold = 0.31

Multiple species maintain stable activity in spatially interleaved regions. Coexistence is sustained because competition is softened by elevated temperature and the lower participation threshold permits weakly concentrated species to remain locally viable.

Findings

Three findings emerge from the calibration process with sufficient generality to warrant independent statement.

finding 1

visual similarity masks mechanistic difference.

Territorial and Edge of Chaos both exhibit spatial borders; Cooperative and Flicker-Mixing both appear spatially mixed. In each pair, the visual resemblance is superficial. Territorial borders settle; Edge borders are subject to ongoing competitive pressure. Cooperative mixture is spatially stable; Flicker mixture is a temporal artifact of rapid rank reversal.

finding 2

diversity preservation must be bounded below, not targeted above.

An aggressive fairness term that targeted equal species shares suppressed competitive asymmetries and produced a uniform, ecologically inert mixed field. Replacing it with a survival floor—marginal support below an extinction threshold only— restored competitive dynamics while preventing trivial monoculture collapse.

finding 3

φ is a designed trajectory, not a universal order parameter.

Frozen, Territorial, Edge, Flicker, and Chaotic regimes can be arranged by monotonically varying instability and selection sharpness. Cooperative behavior requires a non-monotone excursion: elevated temperature combined with a reduced participation threshold. The shape of the φ path encodes this structural asymmetry.

Calibration Protocol

The final φ curve was obtained through an iterative model-identification protocol, not aesthetic tuning: define a regime operationally, reset the ecosystem at the candidate waypoint, observe the failure mode, adjust only interpretable parameters or universal engine dynamics, and retest neighboring regimes to confirm the intervention did not transfer the problem elsewhere.

An automatic regime judge was considered and rejected on grounds of circularity. If "Edge of Chaos" is operationalized as a weighted combination of entropy, spatial autocorrelation, and boundary length, and the system is then tuned to maximize that score, the result validates the score function rather than the phenomenon. The adopted verification criterion is stricter: a waypoint is accepted only when it is visually distinguishable from its neighbors, dynamically interpretable, and traceable to specific values of kgate, τ, and θ through an explicit causal account.

The decisive insight was that each visual failure corresponded to a specific mechanistic ambiguity. Diffuse borders indicated that the growth gate was not creating a sufficiently sharp participation boundary. Stabilized flicker indicated that hard selection existed without the temporal turnover mechanism necessary to prevent winner lock-in. Chaotic states that resembled slow cooperation indicated elevated entropy coupled with unexpectedly high structural persistence. These are not cosmetic distinctions; each implicates a different component of the update rule and constrains which interventions are permissible.

principle i

Parameters define the experimental path; universal dynamics define the substrate. Regime-specific conditionals in the update rule are excluded by design.

principle ii

A regime is accepted only when its failure mode is mechanistically distinguishable from those of its neighbors. Edge of Chaos must fail differently from Territorial; Flicker-Mixing must fail differently from Cooperative.

principle iii

Visual evidence is treated as phenomenological data, then translated into mechanisms: gate sharpness, competition temperature, participation threshold, structural persistence, and frontier mobility.

Discussion

The current result supports a carefully scoped claim: a single scalar can organize a coherent, mechanistically interpretable route through six dynamically distinct regimes of a learned digital ecosystem. It does not establish that the ecosystem possesses a single intrinsic order parameter in the sense of statistical mechanics. φ is a designed experimental trajectory; its validity is empirical and local, not derived from any theoretical reduction of the system's full state space.

The most informative structural feature of the φ path is the non-monotone excursion required for Cooperative behavior. Frozen, Territorial, Edge of Chaos, Flicker-Mixing, and Chaotic regimes can be approximately ordered by increasing competitive instability and decreasing selection sharpness—a progression analogous to the frozen-to-chaotic transition in Langton's framework. Cooperative coexistence, however, is not a continuation of that progression; it requires a qualitatively different combination of high temperature and low threshold. This bend in parameter space has a precise interpretation: stable multi-species coexistence in this system depends on simultaneously softening competitive exclusion and lowering the barrier to participation, rather than simply reducing the strength of competition globally.

The primary open problem is quantitative validation. The six waypoints were defined and accepted on the basis of visual and mechanistic criteria; whether they constitute statistically separable dynamical classes under repeated seeding has not yet been established. Candidate measurements include species diversity and its variance across seeds, spatial autocorrelation length, frontier density, and population-share volatility. Such measurements would not replace the mechanistic definitions, but would test whether those definitions have quantitative content independent of the specific parameter values at which they were calibrated.
